Memex

Vannevar Bush, 1945

Bush imagined a desk-sized device where a researcher stores all their books, records, and communications. The essential feature: associative trails.

"Wholly new forms of encyclopedias will appear, ready-made with a trail of associative trails running through them."

Not hierarchical folders. Not keyword search. Personal paths through knowledge that mirror how the mind actually works — by association, not by index.

02

IPL — Information Processing Language

Newell, Shaw & Simon, 1956

The first list-processing language. Linked lists, symbol manipulation, and the Logic Theorist — the world's first AI program, which proved 38 of 52 theorems from Principia Mathematica.

"For Theorem 2.85, it found a proof shorter and more elegant than Russell and Whitehead's own."

IPL introduced: linked lists, property lists, recursive subroutines, generators, and the idea that intelligence is symbol manipulation.

Symbiosis

J.C.R. Licklider, 1960

Licklider envisioned human-computer symbiosis — not automation that replaces humans, but partnership where each contributes their strengths. Humans set goals and evaluate; computers detect patterns and check consistency.

"The hope is that, in not too many years, human brains and computing machines will be coupled together very tightly."

He also dreamed of an "Intergalactic Computer Network" — and then funded ARPANET to build it.

Xanadu

Ted Nelson, 1960

Nelson's vision: bidirectional links, transclusion (live references instead of copy-paste), and permanent version history. Everything the web got wrong, Xanadu got right first.

"The Web is a trivial, stripped-down version of what we were trying to do. One-way links that break. No transclusion. No version management."

In Xanadu, when you quote someone, the connection is permanent and visible from both sides.

NLS — oN-Line System

Doug Engelbart, 1962

Engelbart's NLS organized all thinking into hierarchical statements with multiple simultaneous views. The same data could be viewed as an outline, as prose, as a network, or filtered by criteria.

"Augmenting human intellect means increasing the capability of a man to approach a complex problem situation."

The "Mother of All Demos" (1968) showed: mouse, hypertext, video conferencing, real-time collaboration — all in one system.

Sketchpad

Ivan Sutherland, 1963

You didn't draw pixels. You sketched rough shapes, then declared constraints: parallel, equal length, perpendicular, on-circle. The system solved for exact positions. The drawing snapped into mathematical perfection.

"Sketchpad was the most important program ever written." — Alan Kay

Ancestor of AutoCAD, SolidWorks, Illustrator, every GUI toolkit, MVC, and object-oriented programming.

07

Logo & Turtle Geometry

Seymour Papert, 1967

The Turtle: a small cursor that children command. FORWARD 100, RIGHT 90. It carries a pen. Geometry becomes body-syntonic — to draw a circle, walk in one yourself.

"You don't evaluate a piano by testing whether pianists are better at mathematics."

The Total Turtle Trip Theorem: any closed path involves turning exactly 360 degrees. Deep mathematics a ten-year-old can discover.

08

PLANNER

Carl Hewitt, 1969

Goal-directed reasoning: state what you want, the system works backwards to figure out how to achieve it. Forward-chaining demons fire when facts are asserted. Backward-chaining proves goals by trying subgoals.

"PLANNER influenced Prolog, the Actor model, and the entire field of AI planning."

The Blocks World: pick up, put down, stack, unstack. Simple operators, yet the planning required to rearrange blocks is surprisingly deep.

Dynabook

Alan Kay, 1972

A live object environment where everything is inspectable and modifiable. Objects communicate only through message-passing. You can redefine any method at any time. The computer is a medium for creation, not consumption.

"The best way to predict the future is to invent it."

Kay envisioned a personal computer for children of all ages — a "Dynabook" that would be a dynamic medium for thought.
